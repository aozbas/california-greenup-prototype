When does California turn green?

Ask most people when plants are greenest and they will say spring. In California, that is only true for part of the state.

We use 26 years of NASA satellite measurements (2000-2025, one image about every 8 days). Greenness is NASA's vegetation index (NDVI): the darker the green, the more plants are growing.

What to take away (dashed lines: 2014)

There is no single green season

  • The coastal hills are greenest in March. The Sierra waits until July.
  • A "good" or "bad" year depends on where you look: in 2014 the hills were far browner than usual while the Sierra was greener.
  • So when you ask whether California is green this year, the answer is: which part?

About this data
  • Source: NASA MODIS vegetation index (NDVI), Terra and Aqua satellites, via NASA GIBS. Each frame is a 16-day composite that keeps every pixel's best observation in the window. Terra's and Aqua's composites are offset by 8 days, so together they give one frame about every 8 days (Aqua from mid-2002).
  • State outline: US Census boundaries (via us-atlas), used only to draw and clip the map.
  • Decoding: GIBS serves colour-coded tiles. NASA's published colour key gives each colour an NDVI range (0.005 to 0.01 wide above 0, about 0.1 wide below 0), and we use the middle of each range, so values are approximate.
  • Resolution: 1 km pixels averaged 2×2 into the 2 km cells you see. Only California land is shown. Terra and Aqua agree to within 0.01 NDVI on average, so they are merged without adjustment.
  • Charts: a place's value is the median of its cells. "Usual" is the median across 2001-2025 (2000 is a partial year) for the same time of year. "Greener/browner" is the difference from it. The shaded band on the chart is the usual range: the middle half of years.
  • Between frames: the map blends the two nearest real frames, so playback looks smooth. There is no daily vegetation data, so the badge says when you are on a real observation.
  • Left out: two composites that were clearly broken (Terra, Apr 22 2000, with 38% of pixels empty, and Aqua, Mar 30 2022, with a statewide dip to 0.11 between neighbours at 0.40), found with an automatic check. The map blends across the gap.
  • Limits: clouds, snow and haze can still dip a composite. NDVI at or below 0 (snow, water, bare ground) is kept as a real value.

Prototype write-up

What have we done so far?

We took the explorer from Project 3 and added a scrolling story that starts by asking if spring is really California's green season. The story has seven short text cards on the left while the map and charts stay on the right. As you scroll the page changes what it shows automatically, and scrolling back brings you to an earlier step. We start with a normal year in late March, then play the map forward to show the coastal hills turning brown by August. After that we show how the Sierra Nevada peaks around July 20, followed by the unusually green spring of 2005 and how differently the Coast Range and Sierra looked in 2014. The last step lets people use the full explorer themselves. While checking the numbers for the story, we caught a mistake from Project 3 and found that the Coast Range actually peaks around March 22 instead of early March once we include data from both satellites. We also built a more polished version with a guessing question and side-by-side maps, but we're saving that for the final.

What will be the most challenging part of our project to design, and why?

The hardest part will probably be making the scrolling story work well with the full explorer. Right now all the buttons, region tiles, and timeline are visible while you read the story, so it gets kind of hard to know where to look. We want to clean up the story view while still making the switch to the full explorer at the end feel natural. Adding labels to the map and charts is also tricky because each step changes what is being shown, so a label that looks fine in one step might cover a line in the next. Small screens are another problem since the pinned map and charts barely fit on a 13-inch laptop. We are also thinking about adding MODIS snow cover data to help explain why the Sierra was greener in 2014, but that means processing another set of satellite images and figuring out how to show the results without making the page too crowded.
